Facts & profile

A charitable hospital's commercial connection was inspected (16/06/2007) and booked for direct theft (two wires tapping the BSES LV mains); a theft bill of Rs 34,23,386 and speaking orders followed, and a Special Court complaint was pending. Its civil suit for a declaration that the bill was false and an injunction against disconnection was rejected as barred; it appealed.

Disposition: Appeal dismissed; the plaint was rightly rejected — though for different reasons than the Single Judge gave (civil court impliedly ousted by Sections 153–154 in theft cases, not by Section 126/145).

Issues framed

  1. whether the concerned matter falls within the jurisdiction of the assessing officer under Section 126 of the Act or not
  2. whether maintainability of such proceeding at the instance of the consumer as discussed above necessarily excludes the jurisdiction of Civil Court

Interpretation of the Electricity Act

Provisions construed: s.126, s.127, s.135, s.145, s.153, s.154(5), CPC s.9 / Order VII rr.10–11

s.145ratiopost-2007 jurisdiction-ouster-scope jurisdiction (§145/§154)
Section 145's express bar on civil-court jurisdiction operates only in respect of Section 126 (assessment) and Section 127 (appeal) matters — not theft under Section 135.
“A perusal of Section 145 would show that this Section operates only in respect of Section 126 and 127 of the Electricity Act.”— para 16
s.126explanatorypost-2007 distinguished-from-135 §126 vs §135
Section 126 excludes mens rea and fastens civil liability; unauthorised use of electricity is different from theft, which is a criminal offence under Section 135.
“Section 126 excludes mens rea and is provided for the purpose of fastening civil liability. Unauthorized use of electricity is different from theft cases of electricity.”— para 15
s.154(5)ratiopost-2007 jurisdiction-forum jurisdiction (§145/§154)
Section 154(5) invests the Special Court with jurisdiction to determine the quantum of civil liability in theft cases; a consumer disputing a theft bill must challenge it before the Special Court, even where no criminal complaint has been filed.
“the provisions of sub-section (5) of section 154 specifically invest Special Court with the jurisdiction to determine any dispute regarding the quantum of civil liability in theft cases”— para 22
s.153/s.154ratiopost-2007 implied-ouster jurisdiction (§145/§154)
Any dispute about civil liability in theft cases is impliedly excluded from the jurisdiction of the civil court by Sections 153 and 154, under which the Special Court determines such liability (acting as both civil and criminal court).
“any dispute about civil liability in theft cases is impliedly excluded from the jurisdiction of civil court in view of the provisions of Section 153 and 154 of the Act”— para 30
s.145ratiopost-2007 complete-code-implied-bar jurisdiction (§145/§154)
The Electricity Act is a complete code; the civil court's jurisdiction is by necessary implication barred, and a consumer cannot approach a civil court without exhausting the alternative remedies the Act provides.
“The scheme of the Electricity Act is complete in itself and thereby the jurisdiction of the civil court to take cognizance of the cases under the Act, by necessary implication, stood barred.”— para 28

Authorities cited

Sohan Lal v. North Delhi Power Ltd. 113 (2004) DLT 547 · Delhi High Court (SB, S.K. Kaul J) followed
Cited for: Section 126 (unauthorised use) and Section 135 (theft) are separate expressions with their own connotations; theft has a dual requirement — a dishonest act plus one of the specified acts in Section 135(1).
“There is dual requirement. Firstly, the act should be done dishonestly. Secondly, it should form par of one of the acts specified in Sub-paras (a) to (c) of Sub-section (1) of Section 135 of the said Act.”— as quoted in the judgment
“This distinction has been made out in a case decided by the High Court of Delhi in Sohan Lal vs. North Delhi Power Ltd.”— the Court’s own treatment
Dhulabhai v. State of Madhya Pradesh AIR 1969 SC 78 · Supreme Court followed
Cited for: The classic seven propositions on when a statute ousts civil-court jurisdiction — including that finality plus an adequate remedy excludes the civil court, save for non-compliance with the Act or breach of fundamental judicial procedure.
“Where the statute gives a finality to the orders of the special tribunals the civil courts jurisdiction must be held to be excluded if there is adequate remedy to do what the civil courts would normally do in a suit.”— as quoted in the judgment
“The relevant principles regarding ouster of jurisdiction of Civil Court were laid down by the Apex Court in Dhulabhai v. State of MP”— the Court’s own treatment
Secretary of State v. Mask & Co. AIR 1940 PC 105 · Privy Council followed
Cited for: Even where jurisdiction is excluded, civil courts may examine cases where the Act's provisions have not been complied with or the statutory tribunal has not conformed to fundamental judicial procedure.
“even if jurisdiction is so excluded, the Civil Courts have jurisdiction to examine into cases where the provisions of the Act have not been complied with, or the statutory tribunal has not acted in conformity with the fundamental principle of judicial procedure”— as quoted in the judgment

Significance

A leading Delhi Division-Bench authority on forum/jurisdiction in electricity disputes: it distinguishes Section 145's express bar (confined to Section 126/127) from the implied ouster of civil courts in theft cases under Sections 153–154, holding that a consumer must take a disputed theft bill to the Special Court. Cited 110 times; it complements Anis Ahmad (consumer fora) on the civil-suit side, applying the Dhulabhai ouster principles.
