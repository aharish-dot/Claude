K.C. Ninan v. Kerala State Electricity Board & Ors.

Nature of Proceeding
Batch of nineteen civil appeals against High Court orders determining whether electricity arrears of a previous owner/occupier can be recovered from a subsequent purchaser (auction purchaser) of the premises, including appeals from Kerala, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Assam, and West Bengal. Reference to 3-judge Bench dated 24 August 2006 (in Maharashtra State Electricity Board v. Super & Stainless Hi Alloys Ltd.), with further matters tagged by order dated 1 November 2007.
Parties
Auction purchasers of premises and subsequent transferees (K.C. Ninan and others) v. Kerala State Electricity Board (KSEB), Maharashtra State Electricity Board (MSEB), Gujarat Electricity Board and distribution licensees (GSEB, PGVCL), Assam Electricity Regulatory Commission (AERC), West Bengal Electricity Regulatory Commission and distribution licensees (WBERC)
Provisions
Sections 2(15), 22, 24 of Electricity Act, 1910; Sections 5, 26, 49, 70(2) of Electricity (Supply) Act, 1948; Sections 2(15), 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 50, 56, 181(2)(x) of Electricity Act, 2003; Section 100 of Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Sections 17, 59 of Indian Registration Act, 1908; General Clauses Act, 1897; Various State Electricity Supply Code regulations (Kerala, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Assam, West Bengal); Conditions of Supply framed by Electric Utilities; Entry 53, List II, Seventh Schedule of Constitution of India; Article 142, Constitution of India
Result
Appeals partly allowed. The validity of Electricity Supply Codes, Conditions of Supply, and subordinate regulations requiring clearing of previous owner’s arrears as a precondition to reconnection or fresh connection is upheld. However, in exercise of discretionary powers under Article 142 of the Constitution, the Court directed the Electric Utilities to waive the outstanding interest accrued on the principal dues from the date of application for supply of electricity by the auction purchasers. Pending applications disposed.

Point for Determination

  1. Whether the Universal Service Obligation under Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003 is an absolute duty to supply electricity, or whether it is subject to compliance with statutory conditions stipulated by the distribution licensee and conditions prescribed under the Electricity Supply Code.
  2. Whether a connection of electricity supply sought by an auction-purchaser (or subsequent transferee) of premises constitutes a reconnection (reviving the original consumer relationship) or a fresh connection (creating a new consumer relationship), and what are the implications of this classification with respect to recovery of arrears.
  3. Whether the power to recover arrears of electricity dues of a previous owner or occupier from an auction-purchaser or subsequent transferee of premises falls within the regulatory authority of State Commissions under Section 50 read with Section 181(2)(x) of the Electricity Act, 2003.
  4. Whether the power to enable recovery of arrears of the previous owner from an auction-purchaser can be provided through subordinate legislation (Electricity Supply Code, Conditions of Supply, or regulations) framed by the State Commissions or Electric Utilities, or whether such power can only be created by express provision in the principal statute.
  5. Whether the Electricity Act, 1910, the Electricity (Supply) Act, 1948, or the Electricity Act, 2003 contain express provisions enabling the creation of a charge or encumbrance over premises for electricity dues.
  6. Whether the statutory bar on disconnection after two years under Section 56(2) of the Electricity Act, 2003 extends to bar other civil remedies of the Electric Utilities to recover such arrears, and whether such limitation impedes conditions of supply requiring clearance of past dues.
  7. What is the implication of an auction-sale of premises on an “as is where is, whatever there is and without recourse” basis with respect to electricity arrears, including whether such a sale places auction purchasers on notice of electricity dues and whether such dues can be fastened on the transferee.

Facts

A batch of nineteen cases involving similar factual patterns: (1) premises had electricity connections under previous owners who failed to pay electricity consumption charges; (2) the Electric Utilities disconnected supply for non-payment; (3) the premises were sold in public auction sales (generally on an ‘as is where is’ basis) to recover loans or dues; (4) the new auction-purchasers applied for reconnection or fresh electricity connections; and (5) the Electric Utilities refused supply unless the auction-purchasers cleared the outstanding dues of the previous owner, relying on Electricity Supply Codes, Conditions of Supply, or state regulations notified in exercise of statutory power.

The High Courts of Kerala, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Assam, and West Bengal reached divergent conclusions: some directed the Electric Utilities to grant connections to auction purchasers without insisting on payment of old arrears (relying on Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board), while others upheld the Utilities’ right to demand clearance of arrears as a condition precedent under the applicable Electricity Supply Code.

By reference order dated 24 August 2006, this Court (in Maharashtra State Electricity Board v. Super & Stainless Hi Alloys Ltd.) referred these appeals to a bench of three Judges to determine whether electricity dues constitute a charge on the property and whether the arrears of the erstwhile owner can be recovered from the subsequent transferee.

By order dated 1 November 2007, other matters of similar nature were tagged along with the reference. The appeals involved Conditions of Supply in Kerala (Condition 15(e)), Maharashtra (Clause 23(b)), Gujarat (amended Clause 4.1.11), Assam (Clause 3.6), and West Bengal (Clause 3.4.2) regulations.

Reasoning of the Court

Headnote The duty to supply electricity under Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003 is not absolute but is subject to the charges and compliances stipulated by the distribution licensee as part of the application for supply, and is subject to conditions prescribed in the Electricity Supply Code framed under Section 50 by the State Commission. The right of supply is with respect to the premises, not the consumer; the synergy between consumer and premises determines the classification of a fresh connection versus reconnection. Conditions of supply framed by Electric Utilities and Electricity Supply Codes are statutory in character when framed in exercise of powers delegated under Section 49 of the 1948 Act or Section 50 of the 2003 Act. The State Commission has wide regulatory power under Section 50 read with Section 181(2)(x) to specify conditions for recovery of electricity arrears of previous owners from subsequent owners. Electricity dues, though not expressly mentioned as creating a ‘charge’ in the principal Acts, can be made subject to a statutory charge through properly framed regulations authorized under the parent statute, provided such charge is not arbitrary or unreasonable. The limitation of two years under Section 56(2) applies to disconnection for non-payment but does not bar civil suits for recovery or conditions of supply requiring clearance of arrears before reconnection. An “as is where is” auction sale places bidders on notice of all liabilities attaching to the premises, including electricity dues prescribed in the Supply Code. Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board is distinguished: it applied only where no statutory regulation or charge was in place; it is inapplicable where statutory regulations validly create liability for previous owner’s dues on the subsequent transferee.

Section 43 duty to supply is subject to statutory conditions and compliance requirements. Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003 casts a statutory duty on every distribution licensee to supply electricity to the owner or occupier of any premises within one month of receipt of a complete application. The provision opens with “Save as otherwise provided in this Act” and explicitly requires the applicant to submit documents showing “payment of necessary charges and other compliances” as determined by the distribution licensee. Section 45 empowers the distribution licensee to fix charges in accordance with tariffs set by the State Commission; Section 46 permits recovery of expenses for providing electric lines or plants; Section 47 authorizes requiring security deposits and refusal of supply for failure to provide security; Section 48 permits stipulation of reasonable terms and restrictions. Section 50 empowers the State Commission to specify an Electricity Supply Code providing for recovery of electricity charges. Reading Sections 43–50 together, the right to obtain electricity supply is not absolute but is conditioned on payment of charges, provision of security, and compliance with terms and conditions prescribed by the distribution licensee or the Electricity Supply Code. The duty to supply is subject to such conditions being satisfied. ¶ 28–36

Supply of electricity is with respect to premises, creating a synergy between consumer and premises. Section 2(15) of the 2003 Act defines “consumer” as any person supplied with electricity for his own use and includes “any person whose premises are for the time being connected for the purpose of receiving electricity.” The provision makes premises central to the definition: the consumer is identified by reference to the premises to which electricity is supplied. The distribution licensee must maintain infrastructure (electric lines, meters, etc.) at identified premises. Section 43 casts the duty on the licensee to supply “to the premises” of an applicant, not merely to a consumer. This creates a synergy: a consumer relationship exists only with respect to particular premises for which electricity is sought and supplied. If the same consumer approaches for electricity at different premises, that constitutes a fresh connection, not a reconnection. Conversely, if different consumers seek supply at the same premises for which electricity was previously supplied, it is a “reconnection” (revival of supply to the premises) if the applicant seeks the same service, but a fresh connection if seeking a new service at those premises. ¶ 29, 62–67

Conditions of supply framed by Electric Utilities are statutory in character when authorized by law. Prior to the 2003 Act, Section 49 of the 1948 Act empowered Electricity Boards to supply electricity on such “terms and conditions” as the Board thought fit, and Section 79 authorized the Board to make regulations not inconsistent with the Act. In Hyderabad Vanaspathi Ltd. v. A.P. State Electricity Board, a 3-judge Bench held that conditions of supply framed by Electricity Boards under Section 49 are statutory in character, not merely contractual. Under the 2003 Act, the State Commission is empowered under Section 50 read with Section 181(2)(x) to specify an Electricity Supply Code providing inter alia for recovery of electricity charges, intervals for billing, and disconnection. The Electricity Supply Code is a subordinate legislation framed after detailed procedures by an independent regulator. Such codes have statutory character and bind all consumers and distribution licensees. A condition of supply requiring a new owner to clear the electricity arrears of the previous owner, if framed in exercise of this statutory power and subject to reasonableness, constitutes valid statutory regulation, not an arbitrary condition. ¶ 19, 69–71

The regulatory power of State Commissions under Section 50 is wide enough to provide for recovery of arrears from subsequent owners. Section 50 of the 2003 Act empowers the State Commission to specify an Electricity Supply Code for, among other things, recovery of electricity charges, intervals for billing, and disconnection. The scope of this power is not limited to charging only the immediate consumer but extends to recovery mechanisms prescribed in the regulatory framework. When a regulation provides that outstanding dues constitute a charge on premises and can be recovered from a subsequent owner seeking supply, such regulation is not beyond the scope of Section 50 because it pertains to “recovery of electricity charges.” The rule-making power under Section 181 read with Section 50 is wide and can encompass conditions framing the manner of recovery, including recovery from subsequent owners. Such a regulation, if reasonably framed and not arbitrary, falls within the legitimate regulatory authority. ¶ 86–89

Electricity dues can constitute a statutory charge on premises through regulations framed under delegated authority. Although the principal Acts (1910 Act, 1948 Act, 2003 Act) do not expressly create a “charge” on premises for electricity dues, such a charge can be created through subordinate legislation framed in exercise of delegated power under the parent statute. The Transfer of Property Act, 1882 recognizes charges created by operation of law as well as by act of parties. Under Section 141 of the Bombay Provincial Municipal Corporations Act, 1949, a charge arises by operation of law for municipal dues. Similarly, regulations framed by State Commissions under Section 50 can prescribe that outstanding electricity dues shall constitute a charge on premises. Such a regulatory charge is not a mortgage in the traditional sense (which requires registration under the Registration Act) but a statutory lien or charge arising by operation of the applicable regulations. The condition that arrears shall not be written off and must be cleared before reconnection creates, in effect, a charge on the premises as a matter of law. ¶ 92–103

The limitation under Section 56(2) bars disconnection after two years but does not limit civil suits or conditions of supply. Section 56(2) of the 2003 Act provides that the distribution licensee cannot disconnect supply for non-payment of charges after the expiry of two years from the date on which the charges became due. The Electric Utilities argue this limitation applies only to the power to disconnect, not to the power to recover through other civil remedies or to condition reconnection on payment. The auction purchasers argue the limitation is absolute and bars any recovery after two years. The Court finds that Section 56(2) bars disconnection as a remedy after two years but does not bar a civil suit for recovery or a condition precedent (such as requiring clearance of arrears before reconnection) because reconnection is not a “disconnection” in reverse but a fresh initiation of service to new premises or a new consumer. The right to condition supply on clearance of arrears is a continuing right independent of the limitation period, provided it is exercised at the time of the application for connection. ¶ 114–125

An “as is where is” auction sale places bidders on notice of all liabilities attached to the premises. In public auction sales, premises are typically sold on an “as is where is, whatever there is and without recourse” basis. This language signifies that the seller (the auction authority or court) does not warrant the condition of the property and the buyer assumes all risks and liabilities attaching to the property. Auction purchasers are expected to conduct due diligence, including ascertaining electricity dues and other liabilities, before bidding. When Electricity Supply Codes or Conditions of Supply are published and specify that electricity dues constitute a charge on premises and must be cleared before reconnection, auction purchasers are constructively on notice of this requirement. The stipulation that the sale is on an “as is where is” basis encompasses the liability for electricity dues prescribed in the applicable Supply Code. An auction purchaser who acquires premises knowing or being on constructive notice of outstanding electricity dues cannot later claim that the condition to clear such dues is unreasonable or unfair. ¶ 127–133

Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board is distinguished; it does not apply where statutory regulations create liability for arrears on subsequent purchasers. In Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board (1995 SCC (2) 648), a 3-judge Bench held that in the absence of a charge being created over premises by a statutory regulation, an auction purchaser cannot be asked to clear past electricity arrears as a condition for supply. The Court elucidated that under Section 24 of the 1910 Act, the contract for supply was only between the Board and the previous consumer, and the subsequent purchaser was neither a consumer nor had contractual relationship with the Board. However, Isha Marbles did not have to deal with any statutory rule, regulation, or condition of supply providing for creation of a charge or for recovery of arrears from a subsequent purchaser. Since the 1948 Act and thereafter the 2003 Act, various State Commissions have framed Electricity Supply Codes creating such charges and prescribing such recovery. The present case is distinguishable from Isha Marbles because here statutory regulations do exist, and they validly create a charge on premises and authorize recovery from subsequent purchasers. The ratio of Isha Marbles applies only where no such statutory regulation exists; it is inapplicable where regulations validly impose such liability. ¶ 17, 201, 324

The rule of ejusdem generis and statutory interpretation confirm the validity of regulatory charges on premises. The auction purchasers argued that conditions of supply cannot go beyond what Section 50 explicitly permits and that a condition imposing liability for third-party (previous consumer’s) arrears is outside the scope of Section 50. The Court applied the rule of ejusdem generis to clarify that the phrase “recovery of electricity charges” in Section 50 is not limited to recovery from the person to whom electricity is being supplied but extends to recovery mechanisms provided by the applicable Electricity Supply Code, including conditions precedent to supply. The Regulation 10.5 of the Maharashtra Electricity Supply Code 2005 (providing that unpaid dues constitute a charge on property and can be recovered from transferees) is a valid mode of recovery falling within Section 50’s scope. ¶ 187–199

Interpretation of the Electricity Statutes

Electricity Act, 2003; Universal Service Obligation; Regulatory Powers of State Commissions; Statutory Charges on Premises; Recovery of Arrears from Subsequent Purchasers

Section 43 — Duty to Supply Electricity, subject to conditions

Section 43 casts a statutory duty on distribution licensees to supply electricity within one month of a complete application, but the provision is hedged by the opening words “Save as otherwise provided in this Act” and by the requirement that the applicant provide documents showing “payment of necessary charges and other compliances.” Reading Section 43 in conjunction with Sections 45–48 and Section 50, the Court is of the view that the duty to supply is subject to: (1) payment of all prescribed charges and tariffs; (2) provision of security deposit as determined by regulations; (3) acceptance of terms and restrictions imposed for compliance with regulations made under Section 53 (now applicable standards); and (4) compliance with conditions stipulated in the Electricity Supply Code framed by the State Commission under Section 50. Conditions stipulated in the Electricity Supply Code, including a requirement to clear outstanding electricity dues of the premises before supply is given or restored, are valid conditions that do not violate the statutory duty under Section 43. ¶ 27–37

Section 50 — Scope of Electricity Supply Code; Recovery of Electricity Charges

Section 50 of the 2003 Act empowers the State Commission to specify an Electricity Supply Code providing, among other things, for recovery of electricity charges, intervals for billing, and disconnection. The scope of the term “recovery of electricity charges” is not confined to recovery from the immediate consumer but extends to all mechanisms for recovery prescribed in the Code, including conditions requiring clearance of outstanding dues before reconnection or fresh supply. When a State Commission, in exercise of this power and after due regulatory procedure, frames a regulation providing that electricity dues constitute a charge on the premises and can be recovered from a subsequent transferee seeking supply, such regulation falls within the authority conferred by Section 50. The regulation is not arbitrary if reasonably tailored to ensure recovery of legitimate dues. ¶ 86–89

Section 56(2) — Two-Year Limitation on Disconnection; No bar to civil remedies or conditions of supply

Section 56(2) of the 2003 Act provides that the distribution licensee cannot disconnect supply for non-payment of charges after two years from the date on which the charges became due. This limitation applies to the remedy of disconnection only and does not bar: (1) civil suits for recovery of dues; (2) conditions of supply requiring payment of outstanding dues as a precondition to reconnection or fresh supply; or (3) continuing the right to condition connection on clearance of arrears at the time of application. The limitation is tied to the remedy of disconnection (cutting off supply) and does not extinguish the underlying right to recover dues or to condition future supply. ¶ 114–121

Definition of “Consumer” and Premises — Synergy between consumer and premises determines connection classification

Section 2(15) of the 2003 Act defines “consumer” by reference to a person whose premises are connected for receiving electricity. The definition emphasizes the role of premises as the locus of the consumer relationship. A person becomes a consumer only with respect to those specific premises for which electricity is supplied. If the same person seeks supply at different premises, it is a fresh connection, not a reconnection. If a different person seeks supply at premises where electricity was previously supplied, it is a reconnection if the same service is sought, but a fresh connection if a new service (e.g., higher capacity) is sought. This synergy between consumer and premises means that when an auction purchaser applies for supply at premises previously supplied to another owner, the application is for a fresh connection (creating a new consumer relationship), not a reconnection (reviving the prior consumer relationship). ¶ 62–67

Statutory Charges on Premises — Created by operation of law through regulations; not requiring express provision in principal statute

A charge on premises for electricity dues need not rest on an express provision in the principal statute (1910 Act, 1948 Act, or 2003 Act) but can be created by properly framed regulations authorized under the parent statute. When the State Commission or the State Government, in exercise of power delegated under Section 49 of the 1948 Act or Section 50 of the 2003 Act, frames a regulation providing that outstanding electricity dues shall constitute a charge on premises, such charge arises by operation of law and is enforceable against subsequent purchasers. The charge is distinct from a mortgage (which requires registration under the Registration Act) and operates as a statutory lien or encumbrance arising from the regulatory prescription. The condition that dues must be cleared before reconnection or fresh supply effectively constitutes a charge because it prevents transfer or continuance of supply without satisfaction of the claim. ¶ 92–103

Conditions of Supply — Statutory character when framed under Section 49 of 1948 Act or Section 50 of 2003 Act

Conditions of supply framed by Electric Utilities or State Commissions in exercise of authority delegated under Section 49 of the 1948 Act (as established in Hyderabad Vanaspathi) or under Section 50 of the 2003 Act are statutory in character and bind all consumers and licensees. Such conditions, when reasonably framed and not arbitrary, have the force of law and can be enforced as conditions precedent to supply. A condition requiring a new owner to clear the electricity arrears of the previous owner, if it is prescribed in the applicable Electricity Supply Code and is not arbitrary or discriminatory, constitutes a valid statutory condition. ¶ 69–71

Ratio Decidendi The appeals are partly allowed. First, the duty to supply electricity under Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003 is not absolute; it is subject to charges and compliances stipulated by the distribution licensee and conditions prescribed in the Electricity Supply Code framed by the State Commission under Section 50. Second, Electricity Supply Codes and Conditions of Supply framed in exercise of authority under Section 49 of the 1948 Act or Section 50 of the 2003 Act are statutory in character and validly require new owners of premises to clear the outstanding electricity dues of previous owners as a condition precedent to reconnection or fresh supply. Third, electricity dues, though not expressly mentioned as creating a “charge” in the principal enactments, can constitute a statutory charge on premises through properly framed regulations; such charge arises by operation of law and is enforceable against subsequent transferees. Fourth, the two-year limitation under Section 56(2) applies to disconnection for non-payment but does not bar civil remedies for recovery or conditions of supply requiring clearance of arrears before reconnection. Fifth, an “as is where is” auction sale places bidders on notice of all liabilities prescribed in the applicable Electricity Supply Code, including electricity dues. Sixth, Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board is distinguished and inapplicable where statutory regulations validly create liability for previous owner’s arrears on the subsequent purchaser. However, in exercise of discretionary jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution and taking into account the lapse of more than two decades in litigation and the hardship to auction purchasers, the Court directed Electric Utilities to waive outstanding interest accrued on principal dues from the date of application for supply. ¶ 328, 327, 324–326

Also Held & Obiter

  • The burden of operation and maintenance costs of electricity infrastructure falls on all consumers if not recovered from defaulting consumers or their successors. The Electric Utilities submitted that if liability for arrears is not fastened to the premises (as opposed to the immediate consumer), the operation and maintenance costs of the infrastructure maintained by the licensee to supply electricity to the premises would ultimately be borne by all other consumers, as such costs would be factored into the fixation of tariff. This consideration supports the validity of requiring new owners to clear arrears: it prevents a cost-shifting to innocent consumers. “The liability to the premises ensures cost recovery from those who benefit from the infrastructure without paying for prior consumption.” ¶ 25 (c)(b)(iii)
  • The 2003 Act was enacted to promote electricity industry development while protecting consumer interests; equity demands balancing after prolonged litigation. The 2003 Act was enacted with objectives including promoting efficiency, competition, and protecting consumer interests. However, after more than two decades of litigation in these batches of appeals since 2001-2006, the Court recognized that granting absolute relief to either party would be inequitable. Commercial entities (many auction purchasers) had suffered loss of business due to denial of electricity connections, yet the Electric Utilities had incurred costs in maintaining infrastructure. In balancing these equities, the Court exercised Article 142 discretion to uphold the regulatory regime but waive interest, giving practical effect to both statutory authority and equitable considerations. ¶ 326–327
Disposition The batch of civil appeals are partly allowed. (1) The validity of the Electricity Supply Codes, Conditions of Supply, and subordinate regulations of the respective states requiring clearing of electricity arrears of the previous owner or occupier as a precondition to reconnection or fresh supply of electricity to the new or subsequent owner or occupier of the premises is upheld. (2) The auction purchasers are liable to pay the principal outstanding electricity dues of the previous consumer in accordance with the applicable Electricity Supply Code and conditions of supply, provided such conditions are not arbitrary or unreasonable. (3) In the exercise of the jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution, and taking into account the lapse of more than two decades since the first appeals were filed, the Electric Utilities are directed to waive the outstanding interest accrued on the principal dues from the date of the application for supply of electricity by the auction purchasers. (4) Pending applications, if any, are disposed of. (5) No order as to costs.

Significance

A landmark judgment clarifying the scope of Universal Service Obligation under Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003, and establishing that electricity dues can constitute a statutory charge on premises through regulations framed under delegated authority. Seven major holdings:

  • Section 43 duty to supply is not absolute but subject to statutory conditions and compliances. The Supreme Court rejected the argument that Section 43 of the Electricity Act, 2003 imposes an absolute, unconditional duty on distribution licensees to supply electricity to any applicant. The opening words “Save as otherwise provided in this Act” and the requirement for “payment of necessary charges and other compliances” make clear that the duty is subject to conditions prescribed in the Electricity Supply Code and reasonable terms stipulated by the licensee. This holding is significant for electricity regulation as it balances the universal service objective with operational and financial sustainability of distribution utilities.
  • Electricity Supply Codes are statutory in character and validly authorize recovery of arrears from subsequent owners. State Commissions, in framing Electricity Supply Codes under Section 50 read with Section 181, exercise delegated legislative authority. Such Codes, when properly framed after due regulatory procedure, are statutory in character and bind all consumers. A Code provision requiring new owners to clear previous owner’s arrears as a condition precedent to supply is a valid regulatory condition falling within the scope of “recovery of electricity charges” under Section 50. This clarifies that regulation-making authority under the 2003 Act is broad enough to address arrears recovery mechanisms and is not limited by Isha Marbles.
  • Electricity dues can constitute a statutory charge on premises through regulations, without express provision in the principal statute. The Court held that although the Electricity Acts do not expressly create a “charge” on premises for electricity dues, such a charge can arise by operation of law through properly framed regulations authorized under the parent statute. This holding recognizes that statutory charges can be created through subordinate legislation and do not require express provision in the principal enactment. The practical effect is that when an Electricity Supply Code provides that dues constitute a charge on premises and must be cleared before reconnection, such provision is valid and enforceable.
  • The two-year limitation under Section 56(2) applies to disconnection remedy only, not to conditions of supply or civil suits. The Court clarified that Section 56(2)’s bar on disconnection after two years does not limit the Electric Utility’s right to condition reconnection on clearance of arrears, nor does it bar civil suits for recovery. The limitation is remedy-specific and does not extinguish the underlying claim. This interpretation protects utilities’ recovery rights while recognizing the limit on disconnection as a coercive measure.
  • An “as is where is” auction sale places bidders on constructive notice of liabilities prescribed in applicable regulations. The Court held that when premises are sold on an “as is where is” basis in public auctions, the terms of such sale place bidders on notice that they must ascertain and account for all liabilities attaching to the premises. When Electricity Supply Codes prescribe that dues constitute a charge on premises, this requirement is deemed incorporated into the auction sale terms. Auction purchasers are expected to conduct due diligence, and a claim of ignorance of electricity dues requirements is not a defense.
  • Isha Marbles is distinguished and does not apply where statutory regulations create liability for arrears. The Supreme Court addressed the tension between Isha Marbles v. Bihar State Electricity Board (which held that without a statutory charge a subsequent purchaser cannot be liable for previous owner’s arrears) and the current regulatory framework (under which Electricity Supply Codes do create such liability). The Court distinguished Isha Marbles on the ground that it decided a case where no statutory regulation creating such liability existed, whereas now such regulations do exist and are valid. This distinction clarifies that Isha Marbles does not bar regulation-making power under the 2003 Act.
  • Equity under Article 142 modulates strict enforcement after prolonged litigation affecting commercial entities. The Court exercised discretionary jurisdiction under Article 142 to waive interest on arrears, notwithstanding the validity of the principal recovery right. The rationale was that after two decades of litigation, auction purchasers (many engaged in commercial operations) had suffered loss of business, and the delay in court proceedings should not compound their hardship. This application of Article 142 demonstrates that even valid regulatory rights can be tempered by equitable considerations in extraordinary circumstances.

Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation v. Haji Abdulgafur Haji Hussenbha
1887300 · High Court (cited with approval by Supreme Court)		 Held (in the context of municipal taxes) that a municipal corporation, which had a charge on property by virtue of law for recovery of tax dues, could recover arrears of taxes from an auction purchaser who had become the owner of the property. The purchaser, who resisted recovery on grounds of lack of notice, was held liable because the charge arose by operation of law, not by notice. The Court cited this case to support the principle that a charge arising by operation of law (statutory charge) for recovery of dues can attach to property and be enforceable against subsequent purchasers even without notice to them. By analogy, a statutory charge for electricity dues arising from the Electricity Supply Code can similarly attach to premises and be enforced against subsequent owners.
