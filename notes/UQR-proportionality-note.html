Interpretive Note · Editorial Commentary

The Unitary Qualifying Ratio — A Boundary Artifact

On the proportionality principle in the second proviso to Rule 3(1)(a), Electricity Rules 2005 — as formulated in M/s. Dakshin Gujarat Vij Company Ltd. v. M/s. Gayatri Shakti Paper & Board Ltd., 2023 INSC 886 [corpus SC‑001], and applied in JSW Energy Ltd. v. Karnataka Electricity Regulatory Commission, High Court of Karnataka, 12 June 2026 [corpus HC‑007].
Nature of this note This is analytical commentary prepared on the digest corpus. It is not a statement of any court’s holding or ratio. Where it argues that a result is right or wrong, that is the author’s reasoned interpretation, expressly identified as such and offered for verification against the judgments themselves.
Thesis The “every 1% of ownership → 1.96% of consumption” formulation (the Unitary Qualifying Ratio, UQR) is a boundary artifact. It is self‑consistent only where the captive users hold ~26% of the plant in aggregate, and it over‑counts the required consumption the moment they hold more — disqualifying users who plainly satisfy the 26% ownership floor, the 51% consumption floor, and proportionality. Read faithfully, the UQR must yield to Rule 3’s aggregate thresholds and to the share‑of‑51% distribution method the Supreme Court expressly adopted from Kadodara.

1. The proposition under scrutiny

The second proviso to Rule 3(1)(a) sets three conjunctive requirements for a group‑captive (association‑of‑persons) structure: the captive users must (i) hold ≥ 26% of the ownership in aggregate; (ii) consume ≥ 51% of the annual generation in aggregate; and (iii) do so in proportion to their shares in ownership, within a variation not exceeding ±10%.

In Dakshin Gujarat (para 43) the Court, adopting Mr. Ramachandran’s illustration, distilled requirement (iii) into a single ratio:

UQR = 51% ÷ 26% = 1.9615 ≈ 1.96  ⇒  each 1% of ownership carries a consumption duty of 1.96% of generation (band ±10% → 1.764% to 2.156%)

2. Why the ratio breaks above the 26% floor

The UQR is nothing more than the ratio of two independent minima. Summing the per‑user duty 1.96 × ownershipi reproduces the 51% aggregate only when total captive ownership equals exactly 26%. Above that point the fixed 51% floor does not move, but the formula keeps scaling — so it demands the impossible:

Aggregate captive ownershipLiteral UQR dutyAggregate consumption demanded
26%1.96% per 1%51% ✓ (true by construction)
50%1.96% per 1%98% ✗
100%1.96% per 1%196% ✗ (impossible)

Rule 3 fixes 51% as a sufficient floor; it does not rise with ownership. The correct “consumption duty per 1% of the plant” is therefore not the constant 1.96 but a declining quantity:

duty per 1% of ownership = 51 ÷ (aggregate captive ownership %)  —  1.96 at 26%, 1.02 at 50%, 0.51 at 100%

The figure 1.96 silently assumes a thin captive slice — captive users sitting at the 26% minimum while outside investors hold the balance. That is the common group‑captive shape, which is why the elaboration reads plausibly; but it is an assumption, not a universal law, and it fails for any structure in which the captive users own materially more than 26%.

3. The corrective is already inside the judgment

Immediately before stating the UQR, the Court (para 42) adopts Kadodara [corpus HC‑006] — and Kadodara’s method is the antidote. It distributes the 51% qualifying requirement across owners by ownership share and tests each against their own slice within ±10%. Its illustration: on 10,000 kWh generated, 5,100 kWh (51%) must be consumed by the owners; three equal owners each consume ⅓ of 5,100 = 1,700 kWh (17% of generation), band 1,530–1,870 — expressly not assessed on 100% of generation.

That 17% figure is mathematically incompatible with a universal 1.96 floor (which would demand 33% × 1.96 = 65% each). Because the Court said it “agree[s] with the reasoning in Kadodara” and merely wished to “elaborate”, the UQR elaboration must be harmonised downwards to Kadodara — not read to override the very method it endorsed.

Structurally, requirement (iii) is a distributional test — does each user’s share of consumption track their share of ownership? — layered on top of the aggregate 26%/51% floors. The UQR re‑expresses that test as an absolute rate against total generation, and the two coincide only at the 26% point. Purpose confirms the reading: the anti‑gaming discussion (paras 45–47) targets “a 1% or an insignificant shareholder [who] disproportionately uses the electricity” — a consumption‑to‑ownership ratio that is too high. The UQR is thus a floor‑screen against token owners, not a ceiling that punishes substantial, proportional ones.

The rule, corrected Where the captive users own X% in aggregate (X ≥ 26), a user owning xi% must consume at least (xi ÷ X) × 51% of generation, within ±10%, subject to the aggregate consumption being ≥ 51%. The UQR of 1.96 is simply the special case X = 26. Reading 1.96 as invariant demands aggregate consumption above the 51% that the Rule itself declares sufficient, and is therefore self‑defeating.

4. Worked example

Two captive users — 25% / 25% ownership, 30% / 30% consumption
  • Aggregate ownership 50% ≥ 26% ✓  ·  aggregate consumption 60% ≥ 51% ✓
  • Ownership shares 50 : 50, consumption shares 50 : 50 → perfectly proportional ✓
  • Correct per‑user minimum = (25 ÷ 50) × 51% = 25.5%; each consumes 30% → qualifies.
  • Literal UQR: 1.764 × 25 = 44.1% each, i.e. 88% together ✗ — the reductio that disproves the universal reading.

The mischief the Court feared — a small owner who is a big consumer — is the exact opposite of these facts (large owners, modest consumers, ratio 1.2). There is nothing here for the anti‑gaming screen to catch; only the mis‑calibrated formula generates a disqualification.

5. What Karnataka HC decided — and did not

JSW Energy [HC‑007] confronted a different defect. KERC’s Clause 6.7 recomputed the UQR dynamically, against total actual consumption ÷ total ownership (a floating “Y ÷ X”). The High Court held (paras 45–49) that Dakshin Gujarat anchors proportionality to the qualifying 51%, not to actual consumption, so KERC had effectively rewritten Rule 3; it quashed the order (and on a parallel natural‑justice / no‑fresh‑consultation ground) and remitted it.

But in doing so the Court re‑affirmed the “fixed, constant baseline UQR” of 1.96 per 1% of ownership (para 46). It framed the entire contest as qualifying‑51% versus total‑actual‑consumption, and never reached the situation in which aggregate captive ownership exceeds 26% — precisely where the over‑counting bites. The scenario in Part 4 is thus genuinely unaddressed, and arguably re‑entrenched by para 46. Correcting the actual‑vs‑qualifying denominator does not cure a formula whose numerator already scales past 51%.

Bottom line The binding content is Rule 3’s thresholds plus Kadodara’s share‑of‑51% distribution; the 1.96 UQR is a boundary illustration and an anti‑gaming floor, not an operative disqualifier above 26% ownership. A remitted KERC procedure — or any future bench — that applies 1.96 as a universal per‑user multiplier will reproduce exactly the error identified here. That gap is a clean ground for a representation on the remit, or for the next appeal.
