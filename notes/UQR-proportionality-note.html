The Unitary Qualifying Ratio: What Dakshin Gujarat Actually Holds — and Its Hard Edge

On the proportionality principle in the second proviso to Rule 3(1)(a), Electricity Rules 2005 — as formulated and illustrated in M/s. Dakshin Gujarat Vij Company Ltd. v. M/s. Gayatri Shakti Paper & Board Ltd., 2023 INSC 886 [corpus SC‑001], and applied in JSW Energy Ltd. v. KERC, High Court of Karnataka, 12 June 2026 [corpus HC‑007].
Correction — this revision supersedes the earlier draft An earlier version of this note argued that the UQR is a “boundary artifact” valid only at 26% ownership, that the Supreme Court had adopted a “distribute‑the‑51%” method, and that a group clearing the 26%/51% aggregates therefore qualifies. That was wrong. It was written before reading the Supreme Court’s five worked illustrations (para 44), which show the Court applying the ratio as a per‑user minimum at every ownership level and expressly denying captive status to a group that clears both aggregates. This revision states the holding correctly and confines the criticism to what genuinely survives — a policy consequence of the rule, not a misreading of it.
Thesis (corrected) The UQR is not a boundary artifact. Dakshin Gujarat applies it as a per‑user floor: each captive user’s consumption must be at least 1.764% of generation for every 1% of shareholding (1.96% − the 10% variation). Clearing the aggregate 26% ownership and 51% consumption thresholds is necessary but not sufficient — Illustration 5 denies captive status to a group that meets both. The real problem is a different one, and it is a genuine defect of the rule: because the floor scales with each member’s ownership, qualification becomes arithmetically impossible once the captive users jointly own more than ~57%, and it fails substantial co‑owners who share output in exact proportion but at less than 1.96:1.

1. The rule and the ratio

For a group‑captive (association‑of‑persons) plant, the second proviso to Rule 3(1)(a) requires that the captive users (i) hold ≥ 26% ownership in aggregate; (ii) consume ≥ 51% of annual generation in aggregate; and (iii) do so in proportion to their shares in ownership, within ±10%. In para 43 the Supreme Court reduced requirement (iii) to a ratio:

UQR = 51% ÷ 26% = 1.96%  —  every 1% of shareholding requires a minimum consumption of 1.96% of generation, permissible band 1.764% to 2.156% (±10%)

2. How the Court actually applies it — a per‑user floor

The operative words are “minimum consumption of 1.96% … for every 1% shareholding”. In application the lower edge of the band (1.764) is what bites: a member must consume at least 1.764× their ownership; consuming more is never a disqualification (Illustrations 2–3 pass members at ratios of 3–4). The method is two‑step: (a) test each member’s ratio and drop anyone below 1.764; (b) the survivors must still cumulatively hold ≥ 26% and consume ≥ 51%. The five illustrations (para 44) are decisive as to what the Court intends:

2Five users own 7/6/5/4/4 (=26%) → consume 15% each (ratios 2.14–3.75).All qualify

5A own 1% → consume 30% (30); B own 25% → consume 21% (0.84). Together own 26%, consume 51%.None qualify — B fails the ratio; A alone < 26%

Illustration 5 is the one that matters here. The two users clear both statutory aggregates — 26% ownership and 51% consumption — and the Court still holds that neither is a captive user, because B’s consumption‑to‑ownership ratio (0.84) falls below the floor. Aggregate compliance does not carry a member over the per‑user test.

3. The reading the Court did not take

One might read requirement (iii) as merely distributing the 51% requirement across owners by their shares (so three equal owners each consume ~17%). That is roughly the Kadodara illustration, and the Supreme Court quoted Kadodara — but only for the narrow proposition that proportionality is assessed against the qualifying 51%, not against 100% of generation. It did not adopt distribution as the test. Illustration 1 forecloses that reading: five owners holding 51% are required to consume 100% (20% each at ratio 1.96), not the 51% that a distribution rule would demand (10.2% each). The per‑user floor, not distribution, is the law.

What the Supreme Court holds Proportionality under the second proviso is a per‑user minimum: each captive user must consume at least 1.764% of generation for every 1% of its shareholding. Meeting the 26% and 51% aggregates is necessary but not sufficient (Illustration 5). Over‑consumption relative to ownership never disqualifies; under‑consumption does. “Gaming” by a token (e.g. 1%) shareholder is caught not by any ratio ceiling but by step (b) — the survivors must still cumulatively hold 26%, which a lone 1% owner cannot.

4. The hard edge — the criticism that survives

The legitimate objection is not that the Court misread the rule; it is that the rule, so applied, produces results that are hard to defend for genuinely captive, high‑ownership structures — and the Court appears to have accepted them deliberately.

The arithmetic ceiling
  • Each member needs consumption ≥ 1.764 × ownership. Summed, the members need total consumption ≥ 1.764 × (aggregate captive ownership).
  • Total consumption cannot exceed 100%. So once captive users jointly own more than ~57% (1.764 × 56.7 ≈ 100), it is arithmetically impossible for all of them to clear the floor — some must be excluded, shrinking the qualifying ownership.
  • Two equal partners owning 50% each and consuming 50% each of their own plant — ratio 1.0 — both fail. To qualify they would each need to consume ~53%, i.e. 106% together: impossible. The more of the plant the captive users own, the harder the test becomes.
Worked example — two users, 25% / 25% ownership, 30% / 30% consumption
  • Aggregate ownership 50% ≥ 26% ✓; aggregate consumption 60% ≥ 51% ✓; consumption shared in exact proportion to ownership ✓.
  • But each ratio = 30 ÷ 25 = 1.2, below the 1.764 floor — so under Dakshin Gujarat both are disqualified (the same defect as B in Illustration 5).
  • This is the Court’s intended result, not an oversight: a substantial owner who under‑consumes relative to its stake is treated as an investor, not a captive user.
Bottom line — for an advocate Do not argue that clearing 26% + 51% is enough; Illustration 5 defeats that. To obtain relief for a high‑ownership captive group, the line is that the per‑user floor yields manifestly unreasonable results for the paradigm captive case (owners who consume most of their own output are disqualified; >57% ownership is unqualifiable), and that the five illustrations are explanatory rather than part of the binding ratio — an uphill submission, but the only honest one. Note that JSW Energy (Karnataka HC) does not help on this: it reaffirmed the fixed 1.96 baseline and decided only that KERC could not swap the qualifying denominator for actual consumption. The hard edge remains unlitigated.
